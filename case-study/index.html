Stepping into the Steamy Kitchen: Why Standard SaaS Dashboards Fail Cooks

Standard dashboards show past data. Kitchen passes show present action. When 80 orders flood in over a single hour across three virtual brands, the physical kitchen pass breaks down.

Before this solution, the kitchen operated on physical app tablets, wet paper chits, and constant verbal shouting. The operational consequences were severe:

From Ingestion to Handover: The 4-Column Operational Journey

To understand the final working solution, let us walk through the happy path of a customer’s order through the kitchen. The system organizes this lifecycle into a structured 4-column workflow.

Column 2 · Cooking Now
SLA countdown Hold / Prep controls Auto-packing Priority sorting
Column 3 · Station Queues
Hot Queue Grill Queue Assembly Queue Bulk cook
Column 4 · Handover

1
Column 1 — “Just Came In”
Order Ingestion
User Goal: Instantly calculate if the kitchen can prepare the order before committing.

The order lands in the system. If Auto-Accept is toggled OFF (manual triage mode), the ticket appears in Column 1 with a strict auto-cancel countdown timer. Subdued, italicized tags alert the manager if a required station is currently overloaded, while a green badge highlights potential batch-cooking opportunities (e.g., ⚡ Batch Match: 2x Fries already cooking). The manager clicks ✓ Start This Order.

Column 2 — “Cooking Now”
SLA-Prioritized Cooking
User Goal: Prepare different items in the same ticket so they finish cooking at the exact same moment.

Once accepted, the ticket immediately moves to Column 2 and is sorted dynamically. Tickets with the least remaining preparation SLA are pinned strictly to the top. Fast-prep items are automatically placed on HOLD by the system. The manager lets the cook prepare the slow-prep item first, releasing the held fast item only when the timers align.

Column 3 — “Station Queues”
Cook Execution
User Goal: Let cooks focus purely on food quality without micromanaging order delivery.

Individual items are stripped from the parent ticket and sent directly to the dedicated vertical tracks in Column 3: Hot Station, Grill Station, or Assembly Station. Cooks read their vertical queue. Every item shows its parent Order ID and overall SLA countdown. When a cook finishes preparing an item, they tap Done on their station screen. The item automatically checks itself off on the master ticket in Column 2.

Column 4 — “Handover / Up for Grabs”
Packing & Hands-Off Handover
User Goal: Hand the completed package to the correct delivery partner with zero wait time.

Once all items on a ticket are checked off as Ready, the ticket background turns into a soft gold wash and moves to Column 4 under Packed & Waiting. Arriving riders are listed in a compact grid showing their statuses. If the assigned rider is already marked 🍺 HERE NOW, the system executes a Zero-Click Auto-Handover, clearing the ticket from the board and triggering a celebratory delivery toast.

The Core Decision Stories: Five Turning Points That Tamed the Chaos

These are the five architectural decisions that had the highest leverage on the system’s ability to perform under real dinner-rush pressure.

Decision Story 1 · The Station Queue System
Why I decided to show prep items in station-wise queues instead of keeping them purely inside individual order cards.
Decision Story 1 — GIF Placeholder Manager hovers over an active order card in Column 2. Corresponding items in Column 3 highlight with a matching border. Shows manager using move buttons to reorder the Hot Station queue. 🎥 16:9 GIF · Station Queue Demo
1
When I first designed the dashboard, I kept all the food items directly under their individual order cards. But when multiple orders started flooding the screen, I realized this was creating too much visual noise for the manager.
2
With 15 different cards on the screen, managing timings and prioritizing tasks became incredibly chaotic. An item at the top of the column might be the exact same as an item at the bottom, forcing the manager to constantly shift their eyes between cards.
3
This manual searching and matching took up all their time, leaving less time for actual decision-making and action — directly causing prep delays. So, I decided to build a dedicated Station-Wise Queue System.
4
Now, when orders arrive, the parent card stays on the main pass for tracking, but the individual items automatically distribute to their respective station lanes (Hot, Grill, or Assembly). Inside the station queue, every item explicitly displays its parent Order Number and overall SLA countdown timer.
5
To give the manager absolute control, I added simple arrow buttons and drag-and-drop mechanics so they can comfortably move items up or down the queue depending on urgency.
6
I also added a Prep Together option. When similar orders arrive, the system prompts the manager that there is an opportunity to cook them in one bulk batch. And if we are preparing four identical items together but need to rush three out immediately, the manager can manually hold a specific item back out of the batched group.
7
Once an item is completed and leaves the station queue, it is automatically marked as finished inside the parent order card. By moving these items into dedicated station queues, the manager can recognize patterns and take actions in seconds, directly increasing kitchen speed and helping deliver orders faster.
Result
The kitchen gained 100% visual traceability. Cooks can look at their station line and see their prep sequence without the manager yelling, and the manager can monitor physical station loads on sight to prevent bottlenecks.
Decision Story 2 · The Anti-Wastage Loop
“Up for Grabs” — How I designed a recovery pool to save prepared food and reduce kitchen waste.
Decision Story 2 — GIF Placeholder Order cancellation event triggers in Column 2. Item transitions smoothly into the Column 4 “Up for Grabs” pool. New order lands, showing the golden “Use Up for Grabs” trigger. 🎥 16:9 GIF · Up for Grabs Recovery
1
I actually thought of this feature during the very last stage of the project. Initially, I had planned canceled-order handling as an “out-of-scope” item because I wanted to keep the project scoped down. But the reality of a busy kitchen is that order cancellations happen frequently in the middle of active preparation.
2
In my early drafts, when an aggregator API sent a cancellation, the ticket was simply deleted from the screen. But that felt wrong. If I am the kitchen manager, my immediate thought isn’t just about the API update; it’s about the physical food that is already prepped or cooking. Completely erasing the card meant we were letting good, prepped food go to waste.
3
So, I came up with a salvage mechanism called the Up for Grabs pool. Now, when an order gets canceled mid-prep or after it’s cooked, the system moves those items into a dedicated “Up for Grabs” section in Column 4.
4
Food safety and freshness are critical for customer ratings, so I added a strict constraint: canceled items only stay in the pool for 30 simulated minutes. An active, visual countdown timer tracks the freshness of each item in the pool, automatically purging them once they hit the 30-minute mark.
5
If a new order lands requiring that exact item within the 30-minute window, a subtle prompt appears on the new card: ↻ Fulfill from Canceled. With a single click, the manager can fulfill the new order from the canceled stock, immediately marking the item as Ready on the new ticket.
6
This completely bypasses the cook stations, converting a costly cancellation event into an instant delivery speed booster for a new customer.
Active Order Canceled
→
Items → “Up for Grabs” Pool
→
30-Min Decay Timer Starts
→
New Order Matched & Re-assigned
Result
This single loop recovered prepped canceled stock, cutting preparation times for matching incoming orders to zero and saving operational food waste — directly recovering margin on every incident.
Decision Story 3 · Order State Controls & Rejection Safeguards
Why I built a dual-acceptance system and double-tap failsafes to prevent expensive aggregator penalties and accidental misclicks.
Decision Story 3 — GIF Placeholder Toggling Auto-Accept OFF reveals the incoming triage feed. Clicking Decline opens the centered, spacious Rejection Modal. Single tap on Cancel does nothing; a double-tap triggers cancel. 🎥 16:9 GIF · Dual-Accept & Safeguard Demo
1
In a high-pressure cloud kitchen, order state transitions are not just casual app clicks — a delayed accept or an accidental cancellation costs the restaurant real money. At first, I made the entire acceptance process manual, but during a peak rush, the manager cannot keep up with clicking “Accept” on every incoming order.
2
More importantly, aggregators like Swiggy and Zomato charge penalty fees and drop restaurant ratings if an order is not accepted within a strict 3-minute window. To eliminate this penalty, I built a global Auto-Accept ON/OFF toggle in the header.
3
When Auto-Accept is toggled ON, orders accept automatically via API, bypassing Column 1 entirely to save the restaurant from penalty fees. But when toggled OFF, orders land in the triage queue, allowing the manager to manually review every ticket during low-stock or closing-hour situations.
4
If they cannot fulfill an order, clicking ✕ Turn Away doesn’t just reject it instantly; it opens a centered focus modal forcing them to select a reason before confirming, preventing hasty rejections that could hurt platform standing.
5
I also noticed a major physical hazard: on the active order cards, I had placed the Cancel and Call Rider buttons right next to each other. With greasy fingers moving fast on a mounted touch screen, the risk of accidentally canceling an active order mid-prep was incredibly high.
6
To prevent this, I designed a double-tap mechanic for the cancel action. A single tap does nothing; the manager must double-tap to confirm a cancellation, giving them a physical, split-second safety net without cluttering the screen with annoying pop-up confirmation boxes.
Result
The system is protected against two of the most costly kitchen errors: missing the aggregator acceptance window and accidentally canceling an active mid-prep order.
Decision Story 4 · Stop Apps with Auto-Resume Timer
How I designed a time-controlled “Stop Apps” modal to prevent kitchen overload without risking lost revenue.
Decision Story 4 — GIF Placeholder Manager opens the Stop Apps modal, selecting specific channels. Sets a 30-minute pause duration; active warning banner appears. Demonstrates manual “Resume” overriding the live countdown timer. 🎥 16:9 GIF · Stop Apps & Auto-Resume
1
During peak hours, when orders flood in from multiple apps and pile up across multiple stations, the kitchen can quickly drop into total panic. When a manager is hit with a sudden, massive wave of tickets, they need a clean, structured way to temporarily pause the incoming order flow.
2
At the same time, this pausing mechanism needs to be highly customized, not just a brute-force shutdown. Sometimes you are getting overwhelmed by Swiggy but Zomato is quiet, or you have fulfilled your quota for one platform and need to slow it down.
3
So, I built a simplified Stop Apps modal that lets the manager pause orders platform-by-platform or brand-by-brand. The manager can select specific channels (like Swiggy) and set a targeted pause duration: 15 minutes, 30 minutes, 1 hour, or 2 hours.
4
I got this idea from observing local restaurants in my town during busy Saturday nights and Sunday afternoons. Whenever they get overloaded, they temporarily turn off their aggregators to let their staff clear the backlog — but they often forget to turn them back on, losing hours of potential revenue.
5
To solve this, I designed an Auto-Resume Timer. Once paused, a clear, running countdown timer appears on the main screen, showing exactly when the apps will automatically turn back on. If the kitchen clears its backlog early, the manager can simply click the “Resume” button to start receiving orders immediately.
Result
The manager can pause the inflow, stay calm, and ensure active orders go out hot and on time, while safely capturing new business the moment the kitchen is ready — with zero risk of forgetting to resume.
Decision Story 5 · Out of Stock (OOS) Management
How I designed a 10-item Out of Stock toggle panel to prevent the kitchen from accepting orders we cannot physically prepare.
Decision Story 5 — GIF Placeholder Opening the OOS modal and selecting the “Patty & Bun” brand. Toggling “Classic French Fries” to OFF. New incoming order with Fries is held and flagged with an alert. 🎥 16:9 GIF · Out of Stock Manager
1
Running out of ingredients mid-rush is one of the most stressful challenges in a kitchen. Normally, if an item goes out of stock, the kitchen doesn’t realize it until the order has already been accepted and the cook looks for the ingredient.
2
This causes immediate panic and confusion. To avoid canceling, many restaurants make silent substitutions (like replacing a chicken patty with a veg one), which directly results in poor ratings and bad reviews.
3
I modeled the flow around a realistic kitchen context: the chef yells to the manager, “We are out of fries!” and the manager takes immediate action on the screen. Instead of forcing the manager to scroll through a giant list of hundreds of items, the modal features a brand-selector dropdown.
4
Selecting a brand instantly loads a clean vertical list of exactly 10 high-velocity items with bold, touch-safe ON/OFF toggles. Once an item is toggled OFF, the system automatically communicates with the aggregator APIs to deactivate it.
5
More importantly, if an order containing that item is already pending in the triage list, the system blocks the Auto-Accept engine and flags the card with a warning: Contains Out-of-Stock Item. This allows the manager to reject the order outright with the correct reason before it ever enters the kitchen, saving valuable prep time and preserving customer trust.
Result
The kitchen can now act on a stock-out in under 10 seconds, preventing misfulfilled orders before they enter the prep pipeline and eliminating silent substitutions that damage platform ratings.

Craft & Execution: How Systematic Tokens and Auto Layout Built a Rugged UI

Willow Kitchen is built on a clean, high-contrast, light-mode visual design inspired by standard restaurant KDS systems — featuring crisp whites, deep oxbloods, and high-visibility typography.

Spacing & Density
To prevent 14+ active tickets from merging into a confusing wall of text, I applied a strict vertical 8pt Spacing Rhythm (--spacing-8, --spacing-16, --spacing-24). Cards are padded generously with 16px of inner margins, and items inside the card use an 8px vertical gap to ensure readability.
Auto Layout & Scalability
Every card, ticket, and modal was built using Nested Auto Layout in Figma. This ensures that when an order’s item list grows or when a “Fulfill from Canceled” prompt appears, the ticket scales dynamically without forcing any manual layout adjustments.

Shift Reflection: What Building Under Pressure Taught Me About Visual Discipline

“Great utility design is quiet. It is not about decorative features, gradients, or aggressive animations — it’s about removing cognitive weight so humans can work with confidence under pressure.”

Shift Reflection — 4-Week Sprint

This project taught me a major lesson about operational design. When a kitchen re-opens and orders flood in, a loud UI is a liability. By strictly rationing color, keeping card backgrounds neutral, and using smooth, in-place text updates, we create an interface that calms the user instead of distracting them.

What I will carry forward into my next project is this: Do not design for the ideal, calm user. Design for the stressed, distracted user working under harsh conditions. That is where real UX maturity shows.

The Unfinished Edge: What I Would Explore with More Time

While the current version of the Willow Kitchen KDS is fully functional, there are two key areas I would explore with more resources.

01
Direct Thermal Printer Integration
Building a direct web-print handshake so that when manual accept is toggled OFF, the system automatically triggers a printed physical chit on the pass — allowing hybrid kitchens to run both digital and paper workflows together simultaneously.
02
Rider GPS Distance Triggers
Instead of relying purely on simulated transit times, integrating live GPS distance APIs from Swiggy and Zomato. The KDS could automatically hold fast-prep items from cooking until the rider’s physical distance falls under a 2-kilometer threshold, ensuring 100% hot food delivery.
