Stepping into the Steamy Kitchen: Why Standard SaaS Dashboards Fail Cooks

Standard dashboards show past data. Kitchen passes show present action. When 80 orders flood in over a single hour across three virtual brands, the physical kitchen pass breaks down.

Before this solution, the kitchen operated on physical app tablets, wet paper chits, and constant verbal shouting. The operational consequences were severe:

The Core Decision Stories: Five Turning Points That Tamed the Chaos

These are the five architectural decisions that had the highest leverage on the system’s ability to perform under real dinner-rush pressure.

DECISION STORY 1 · STATION QUEUE SYSTEM
Why I decided to show prep items in station-wise queues instead of keeping them inside individual order cards.
The Problem

When I tried showing items under individual order cards, it was too much to look at for the manager when trying to organize, prioritize, or cook things together. With multiple orders coming through, it was very chaotic, and an item at the top of the column might be the exact same as an item at the bottom. The manager had to constantly shift their eyes between different cards to manage timings, taking up all their time just to recognize items instead of taking action, directly delaying the orders.

The Design Solution

So, I built a station-wise queue system where items split automatically into dedicated lanes (Hot, Grill, Assembly) based on stations, allowing the manager to track them by order number. I added simple arrow buttons and drag-and-drop so the manager can comfortably move items up or down. I also added a Prep Together option for similar items, with the granular control to hold back a specific item if some need to go out immediately while others can be delayed.

The Result

Once an item is finished cooking and leaves the station queue, it is automatically marked as finished inside the parent order card. This allows the manager to track items based on the order number, take decisions faster, and take actions faster, directly increasing speed in the kitchen and delivering orders on time.

DECISION STORY 2 · CANCELED STOCK RECOVERY
How I use an up for grabs section to reduce prepared food wasteage and also making orders faster.
Decision Story 2 — GIF Placeholder Order cancellation event triggers in Column 2. Item transitions smoothly into the Column 4 “Up for Grabs” pool. New order lands, showing the golden “Use Up for Grabs” trigger. 🎥 16:9 GIF · Up for Grabs Recovery
The Problem

Cancellations happen frequently in the middle of preparation, but in my early drafts, when an aggregator sent a cancellation, the ticket was simply deleted from the screen. If I am the kitchen manager, completely erasing the card means good food goes straight into the trash, causing high food waste and directly hurting restaurant margins. I wanted a system that would prevent this waste by letting the manager reuse canceled, prepped food for other orders.

The Design Solution

I created the “Up for Grabs” section in Column 4 where canceled items sit for exactly 30 minutes, backed by an active countdown bar to ensure food safety and freshness. If a new order arrives requiring that exact item within the 30-minute window, a subtle ↻ Fulfill from Canceled prompt appears on the card, letting the manager decide to reuse it with a single click.

The Result

Food is not wasted, and reusing the canceled item instantly speeds up the new order. The manager can bypass the cook stations entirely, which directly increases kitchen speed and gets new orders delivered faster.

DECISION STORY 3 · ORDER STATE CONTROLS
Why I built an auto-acceptance system and double-tap failsafes to prevent expensive aggregator penalties and accidental misclicks.
Decision Story 3 — GIF Placeholder Toggling Auto-Accept OFF reveals the incoming triage feed. Clicking Decline opens the centered, spacious Rejection Modal. Single tap on Cancel does nothing; a double-tap triggers cancel. 🎥 16:9 GIF · Dual-Accept & Safeguard Demo
The Problem

During peak hours, a manager cannot manually accept every single incoming order when they are flooding the screen, but aggregators like Swiggy and Zomato charge expensive penalty fees if an order is not accepted within a strict three-minute window. Additionally, on the active order cards, I had placed the Cancel and Call Rider buttons right next to each other, creating a high risk where the manager could mistakenly tap the cancel button during a busy rush.

The Design Solution

I built a global Auto-Accept ON/OFF toggle in the header to auto-approve orders and bypass aggregator fees during peak rushes. When turned off, declining an order opens a centered modal to select reasons cleanly, and for cancellations on active cards, I designed a double-tap gesture on the Cancel action to get a physical, split-second reconfirmation.

The Result

The restaurant avoids expensive penalty fees and completely eliminates accidental order cancellations, providing a physical safety net for the manager without slowing down the fast pace of the kitchen.

DECISION STORY 4 · STOP APPS WITH TIMER
How I designed a time-controlled “Stop Apps” feature to prevent kitchen overload in peak hours.
Decision Story 4 — GIF Placeholder Manager opens the Stop Apps modal, selecting specific channels. Sets a 30-minute pause duration; active warning banner appears. Demonstrates manual “Resume” overriding the live countdown timer. 🎥 16:9 GIF · Stop Apps & Auto-Resume
The Problem

During peak hours, too many orders flowing from multiple apps to multiple stations causes panic and kitchen overload. I noticed local restaurants manually stop orders during busy Sunday afternoons when they can't handle the rush, but they often forget to turn them back on, leading to major revenue loss. Standard systems only offer total shutdowns, preventing managers from pausing just one busy app while keeping quieter channels open.

The Design Solution

I designed a “Stop Apps” modal where the manager can pause specific apps or brands for set times (15m, 30m, 1h, 2h) depending on kitchen load. The system runs an auto-resume countdown timer on the screen with a manual override button, letting the manager easily turn the apps back on whenever they are ready.

The Result

The manager can pause the inflow based on requirements and stay calm, ensuring current orders deliver on time. New orders are paused for some time and arrive only when the kitchen is free, so they can also be prepared and delivered on time.

DECISION STORY 5 · OUT OF STOCK MANAGEMENT
How I designed an Out of Stock toggle panel to prevent the kitchen from accepting orders they cannot prepare.
Decision Story 5 — GIF Placeholder Opening the OOS modal and selecting the “Patty & Bun” brand. Toggling “Classic French Fries” to OFF. New incoming order with Fries is held and flagged with an alert. 🎥 16:9 GIF · Out of Stock Manager
The Problem

Running out of ingredients mid-rush is one of the biggest challenges, and usually, restaurants accept orders and only realize they are out of stock after the ticket goes to the kitchen. This causes panic and confusion, often forcing restaurants to make silent ingredient substitutions that reduce customer ratings. Sifting through a massive scrolling list to deactivate an item is too slow.

The Design Solution

Based on a verbal update from the chef, the manager uses a streamlined panel with a brand dropdown and exactly 10 high-velocity toggles to mark items out of stock. New incoming orders containing OOS items are held in the triage list with a warning badge, allowing the manager to reject unfulfillable orders upfront before they enter the kitchen.

The Result

The kitchen avoids wasting time on unfulfillable orders, and customers are notified of the rejection right away instead of receiving wrong, substituted items.

Craft & Execution: How Systematic Tokens and Auto Layout Built a Rugged UI

Willow Kitchen is built on a clean, high-contrast, light-mode visual design inspired by standard restaurant KDS systems — featuring crisp whites, deep oxbloods, and high-visibility typography.

Spacing & Density
To prevent 14+ active tickets from merging into a confusing wall of text, I applied a strict vertical 8pt Spacing Rhythm (--spacing-8, --spacing-16, --spacing-24). Cards are padded generously with 16px of inner margins, and items inside the card use an 8px vertical gap to ensure readability.
Auto Layout & Scalability
Every card, ticket, and modal was built using Nested Auto Layout in Figma. This ensures that when an order’s item list grows or when a “Fulfill from Canceled” prompt appears, the ticket scales dynamically without forcing any manual layout adjustments.

Shift Reflection: What Building Under Pressure Taught Me About Visual Discipline

“Great utility design is quiet. It is not about decorative features, gradients, or aggressive animations — it’s about removing cognitive weight so humans can work with confidence under pressure.”

Shift Reflection — 4-Week Sprint

This project taught me a major lesson about operational design. When a kitchen re-opens and orders flood in, a loud UI is a liability. By strictly rationing color, keeping card backgrounds neutral, and using smooth, in-place text updates, we create an interface that calms the user instead of distracting them.

What I will carry forward into my next project is this: Do not design for the ideal, calm user. Design for the stressed, distracted user working under harsh conditions. That is where real UX maturity shows.

The Unfinished Edge: What I Would Explore with More Time

While the current version of the Willow Kitchen KDS is fully functional, there are two key areas I would explore with more resources.

01
Direct Thermal Printer Integration
Building a direct web-print handshake so that when manual accept is toggled OFF, the system automatically triggers a printed physical chit on the pass — allowing hybrid kitchens to run both digital and paper workflows together simultaneously.
02
Rider GPS Distance Triggers
Instead of relying purely on simulated transit times, integrating live GPS distance APIs from Swiggy and Zomato. The KDS could automatically hold fast-prep items from cooking until the rider’s physical distance falls under a 2-kilometer threshold, ensuring 100% hot food delivery.
